<p>Sometimes you think you understand something, but you fail to explain it. This is the time that you have to look back from a different perspective and start over. <strong>When you dig in medical images you will see different concepts to seem vague and non-intuitive</strong>, at least in the beginning. You will see people discussing DICOM and coordinate systems you have never heard before. As a result, a lot of misconceptions and confusions are born. If you are in this position, or if you would like to know about AI in medical imaging this article is for you.</p>
<p>Back in 2017, when I applied for my master’s degree in biomedical engineering everybody asked me why, as I was already obsessed with deep learning. Now, every multidisciplinary deep learning research project requires domain knowledge such as medical imaging. Interestingly, the funding in the AI Healthcare domain is continuously increasing. As an quantitative example of <a href="https://www.healthcaredive.com/news/ai-based-medical-imaging-market-on-track-to-hit-2b-by-2023/529269/#:~:text=The%20global%20market%20for%20artificial,a%20new%20Signify%20Research%20report." target="_blank" rel="noopener">first google search</a> that one can find out:</p>
<blockquote><p>The market for machine learning in diagnostic imaging will top 2 billion <!-- -->$<!-- --> by 2023.</p></blockquote>
<p>So, <strong>the reason that I decided to write this article is to help ML people dive into medical imaging</strong>.</p>
<p>In a previous <a href="https://theaisummer.com/medical-image-deep-learning/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">article</a>, I talked about a common deep learning pipeline applied to multi-modal magnetic resonance datasets. All of that of course with our under development open source pytorch library called <a href="https://github.com/black0017/MedicalZooPytorch" target="_blank" rel="noopener">medicalzoo-pytorch</a>. However, I didn&#8217;t dive into the particularities of the medical world too much. In the end, I used already processed data from an ML competition (and not from a messy hospital), so somebody else did the dirty work for me. This tutorial is partly based in the nipy <!-- -->[1]<!-- --> and 3D Slicer.org <!-- -->[2]<!-- --> documentations for medical images and Dicom files.</p>
<blockquote><p>To dive deeper into how AI is used in Medicine, you can’t go wrong with the <a href="https://coursera.pxf.io/3PMMrr" target="_blank" rel="noopener">AI for Medicine</a> online course, offered by Coursera. If you want to focus on medical image analysis with deep learning, I highly recommend starting from the <a href="https://tidd.ly/3E2z3Uk" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Pytorch-based Udemy Course</a>.</p></blockquote>

There are a lot of assumptions that ML engineers have no idea about. Other multi-disciplinary projects have this kind of terminology problem. At least, I&#8217;ll try my best, one concept at a time!</p>

For convenience, and to avoid misconceptions we will use the world <strong>modality</strong>, throughout this tutorial to refer to any kind of medical image exam. <p>We are all set to go into the medical imaging world!</p>
<h2 id="the-coordinate-systems-in-medical-imaging">The coordinate systems in medical imaging</h2>
<p>A coordinate system is a method for identifying the location of a point. In the medical world, there are three coordinate systems commonly used in imaging applications: the <strong>world</strong>, <strong>anatomical,</strong> and the <strong>medical</strong> <strong>image coordinate system</strong>.</p>

<h2 id="world-coordinate-system">World coordinate system</h2>
<p>The world coordinate system is a <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate_system" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Cartesian coordinate system</a> in which a medical image modality (e.g. an MRI scanner or CT) is positioned. <strong>Every medical modality has its own coordinate system</strong>, but there is only one world coordinate system to define the position and orientation of each modality.</p>
<h2 id="anatomical-coordinate-system">Anatomical coordinate system</h2>
<p>The most important model coordinate system for medical imaging techniques is the anatomical space (also called the patient coordinate system). This space <strong>consists of three planes to describe the standard anatomical position of a human</strong>:</p>
<ol>
<li>the <strong>axial</strong> plane</li>
<li>the <strong>sagittal</strong> plane</li>
<li>the <strong>coronal</strong> plane</li>
</ol>
<p>It is worth mentioning that this system was invented for better communication between doctors and radiologists.</p>
<blockquote><p>Tip: In order to understand the anatomical planes, you have to <strong>visualize a standing human looking at you</strong>.</p></blockquote>
<p>Moreover, <strong>in this system everything is relevant</strong>. Thus, the 3D position is defined along the anatomical axes of anterior-posterior (front-back) and left-right and inferior-superior, as we will see. By this sense, <strong>all axes have their notation in a positive direction</strong>.</p>
<h3 id="axial-plane">Axial plane</h3>
<p><strong>The axial plane</strong> is actually when you place your point of view above the patient and look down. Depending on the region of the 3D medical image you will observe different anatomical structures. For a 3D total body scan, if you had a control-bar over this 2D view you would start from a 2D slice of the head, and by increasing you would end up in the legs. Let’s practically call this view the “drone plane” or “top-view”. Below you can see different slices of a brain MRI.</p>
<p><img decoding="async" src="https://media.giphy.com/media/Vn9JVHDAzYw1O/giphy.gif" alt="https://media.giphy.com/media/Vn9JVHDAzYw1O/giphy.gif" /></p>
<p>For anatomical consistency reasons, a slice near the head is referred to as <strong>superior</strong> compared to a slice closer to the feet, which is called <strong>inferior</strong>.</p>
<p>Does it seem complex? It is, I know! But it&#8217;s important for your mental sanity if you want to survive in the field. Unfortunately, the other two planes assume different directions for positive 😢.</p>
<p>For completeness, and to present something a little bit more recent below you see an <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CT_scan" target="_blank" rel="noopener">axial CT</a> of a patient. Radiologists have highlighted irregularities that may be due to COVID. I am just resharing the image here to illustrate the axial slice, looking from the top to the patient. The particular slice refers in the lungs.</p>

<h3 id="sagittal-plane">Sagittal plane</h3>
<p>Basically, this is a side view. Instead of looking from above the patient, now we look from the side. The side can be either <strong>right</strong> or <strong>left.</strong> Which side and direction is the positive one, depends on the coordinate system! The sure thing is that from this view (plane) you can see the patient&#8217;s ear! As you move through this axis you see the projected tissues like lungs, bones.</p>
<p>A sagittal view of a brain MRI can be illustrated below:</p>
<p><img decoding="async" src="https://media.giphy.com/media/6kpxY5eEgVfY4/giphy.gif" alt="https://media.giphy.com/media/6kpxY5eEgVfY4/giphy.gif" /></p>

<p>In this view, we traverse either by looking in the eyes (<strong>anterior plane</strong>) or by looking in the back of a patient (<strong>posterior plane</strong>). I hope you get the idea by now.</p>

<h2 id="medical-image-coordinate-system-voxel-space">Medical Image coordinate system (Voxel space)</h2>
<p>This is the part that comes more intuitively for people with a computer vision background. If you have any experience with other 3D deep learning domains, I can assure you that this is the place that you will find some rationality and relevant context, at last!</p>
<p>Medical modalities create 3D <strong>arrays/tensors</strong> of points and cells which start at the upper left corner, similar to an RGB camera for 2D. The <em>i</em> axis increases to the right (width), the <em>j</em> axis to the bottom (height), and the <em>k</em> axis backward (the 3rd similarly to the feature maps of a conv net).</p>
<p>In addition to the intensity value of each voxel <em>(i j k)</em>, the origin (i.e. MRI) and spacing of the coordinates are stored in the meta-data of the medical image (either dicom tags, header file of nifty images, you name it.. ).</p>
<blockquote><p>Voxel spacing is basically the real size of the voxel that corresponds to a 3D region and the distance between two voxels.</p></blockquote>
<p>This is significant if we want for example to measure the volume of a cancer tumor cell.</p>
<p>Note that, it is possible to resample to a bigger voxel size to reduce the size of the medical image. This can be understood as a downsampling operation of a signal. Medical people will say that in this way we keep the field of view the same, but let’s simply say that <strong>it’s a kind of downsampling technique in the voxel space.</strong></p>
<blockquote><p>Note: if the voxels are of the same size in all 3 dimensions we call the image isotropic, similar to isotropic scaling in RGB images.</p></blockquote>
<p>Now that we see all these nice coordinate theories, let’s see how we can manipulate and transform from one world to another.</p>
<h2 id="moving-between-worlds">Moving between worlds</h2>
<p>In order to move from one world to another, we need a magic key: it’s called the well-known <strong>affine</strong> matrix. With this, we can move from one world to another via a so-called affine transformation. But what is an affine transformation?</p>
<h3 id="affine-transformation">Affine transformation</h3>
<p>Before affine transformation let’s clarify what a geometric transformation is. A geometric mapping/transformations is a way to clarify that the voxel intensity does not change.</p>
<p>So, based on the definition of Wikipedia for affine <!-- -->[7]<!-- -->: in geometry, an affine transformation is a <strong>geometric mapping</strong> of an affine space that preserves a lot of properties such as it sends points to points, lines to lines, planes to planes. Furthermore, it also preserves the ratio of the lengths of parallel lines. However, an affine transformation does not necessarily preserve angles between lines or distances between points.</p>
<p>In math, to represent translation and rotation together we need to create a square affine matrix, which has one more dimensionality than our space. Since we are in the 3D space we need a 4D affine matrix in medical imaging. With the affine matrix, we can <strong>represent any linear geometrical transformation</strong> (translation, rotation), <strong>by a matrix multiplication</strong>, as illustrated below.</p>

<p>Now that we briefly covered some coordinate system concepts, let’s discuss the DICOM nightmare!</p>
<p>All modern medical imaging modalities like X-Rays, Ultrasound, CT (computed tomography), and MRI (Magnetic Resonance Imaging) support DICOM and use it extensively. <strong>DICOM is first of all an Interface Definition</strong>. It’s success relies on the ability to integrate medical modalities manufactured by different vendors. And unfortunately for the medical world, this was almost impossible before DICOM. For the record, integrating medical equipment of different vendors used to be a huge issue. Actually, this is the reason that DICOM has naturally become the industry standard.</p>
<p>DICOM is a unique format as it does not only store the medical image data but also <strong>data-sets</strong>, which are made up of <strong>attributes</strong>. For our readers with a software engineering background let’s simply say that it contains a huge amount of meta-data with the image (and usually redundant). The meta-data contain critical information that must be kept within the file to ensure they are never separated from each other.</p>
<blockquote><p>Tip: To summarize, the core of DICOM is both a file format and a networking protocol.</p></blockquote>
<ul>
<li><strong>DICOM File Format</strong>: All medical images can be saved in DICOM format. Medical imaging modalities create DICOM files. The huge adoption of DICOM files is justified because they contain more than just medical images, as explained. Specifically, every DICOM file holds demographic patient information (name, birth date), as well as important acquisition data (e.g., type of modality used and its settings), and the context of the imaging study (i.e. radiotherapy series, patient history). As a machine learning engineer, this is the most critical concept that you need to deal with Dicom files.</li>
<li><strong>DICOM Network Protocol</strong>: However, DICOM does not only define the image and data-sets (meta-data) but also the transport layer protocol, more or less like TCP or any other protocol. The entire standard is made up of multiple related but independent sections. Nevertheless, all medical imaging applications that are connected to the hospital network use the DICOM protocol to exchange information, which is mainly DICOM images. Moreover, the DICOM network protocol is used to search for imaging studies in the archive and restore imaging studies to the workstation in order to display it. Finally, everything complex abstraction comes with a good part: Since DICOM is a complex protocol it allows for multiple commands such as schedule procedures, report statuses.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote><p><strong>Tip</strong>: In medical image analysis we are mostly interested in understanding our data so we can preprocess them to train a deep neural network.</p></blockquote>
<p>From now on, we will refer to DICOM as the file format for simplicity.</p>

<p><span class="token plain"> -v : verbose (n/y or 0/1/2 [no, yes, logorrheic], default 0)</span></p>
<p><span class="token plain"> -x : crop (y/n, default n)</span></p>
<p><span class="token plain"> -z : gz compress images (y/i/n/3, default n) [y=pigz, i=internal:zlib, n=no, 3=no,3D]</span></p>
<p><span class="token plain"> Defaults file : /home/nikolas/.dcm2nii.ini</span></p>
<p><span class="token plain"> Examples :</span></p>

<p><span class="token plain"> dcm2niix -o /users/cr/outdir/ -z y ~/dicomdir</span></p>
<p><span class="token plain"> dcm2niix -f %p_%s -b y -ba n ~/dicomdir</span></p>

<p><span class="token plain"> dcm2niix -o &#8220;~/dir with spaces/dir&#8221; ~/dicomdir</span></p>

<p>I have used this for converting <a href="https://www.radiologyinfo.org/en/info.cfm?pg=pet" target="_blank" rel="noopener">PET</a> images, 4D CT, CT, and even cone-beam CT (CBCT). <strong>The tricky part comes when you want to read labels/annotations</strong>. Of course, there is not a single way to do this and it depends on the problem and the annotation toolbox. As soon as I understand this part better, I will provide more info on this. For the record, usually the ‘-m y’ option is needed to merge the slices regardless of study time for functional imaging. Functional means that the images are not structural, but they have a lot of timesteps.</p>
<blockquote><p>Tip: Structural medical images are like camera images (static), while functional medical images are kind of like videos.</p></blockquote>
<p>The most famous functional imaging is brain <a href="https://cfmriweb.ucsd.edu/Research/whatisfmri.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener">fMRI</a>. As a result, a functional medical image is 4-dimensional. A beautiful way to understand this is by watching this video that associates brain activity based on brain fMRI signals with music.</p>
<p><iframe title="The Tango Brain | How Music Affects the Brain" width="500" height="375" src="https://www.youtube.com/embed/jkMlOrxYqWA?feature=oembed" frameborder="0" allow="accelerometer; autoplay; clipboard-write; encrypted-media; gyroscope; picture-in-picture; web-share" referrerpolicy="strict-origin-when-cross-origin" allowfullscreen></iframe></p>

<p>If you’ve ever used AI-generated content or watched an algorithm predict what you want before you even type a word, you might think machines are getting close to thinking like us. But are they really? AI can process massive amounts of data, detect patterns, and make incredibly accurate predictions, but it doesn’t actually <em>think</em>. Instead, it perceives. And that distinction is critical—not just for how we understand artificial intelligence, but for how we think about curiosity in business and leadership. AI’s ability to recognize and respond to patterns has changed industries, but its limitations highlight something we often take for granted: human curiosity. Deep learning models don’t ask <em>why</em>—they refine probabilities. They don’t <a href="https://www.forbes.com/sites/dianehamilton/2024/12/01/how-to-challenge-assumptions-and-unlock-your-teams-potential-at-work/" data-ga-track="InternalLink:https://www.forbes.com/sites/dianehamilton/2024/12/01/how-to-challenge-assumptions-and-unlock-your-teams-potential-at-work/" target="_self" aria-label="challenge assumptions">challenge assumptions</a>—they reinforce them. In business, this raises an important question: Are we relying so much on AI’s pattern recognition that we’re losing the very thing that makes us uniquely human—our curiosity?</p>
<figure class="embed-base image-embed embed-1" role="presentation"><figcaption><fbs-accordion></p>
<p class="color-body light-text" role="button">AI Is Great At Perception—But Perception Isn’t Thinking</p>
<p></fbs-accordion><small>getty</small></figcaption></figure>
<h2>AI Is Great At Perception—But Perception Isn’t Thinking</h2>
<p>Neural networks don’t “understand” the world in the way we do. They identify correlations, much like an infant noticing that every time a parent walks toward a cabinet, food appears. But understanding <em>why</em> the cabinet holds food, why it’s restocked, and what factors might change that process? That’s beyond AI’s scope.</p>
<p><a href="https://achievement.org/achiever/marvin-minsky-ph-d/" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener noreferrer" data-ga-track="ExternalLink:https://achievement.org/achiever/marvin-minsky-ph-d/" aria-label="MIT professor Marvin Minsky, one of AI’s pioneers">MIT professor Marvin Minsky, one of AI’s pioneers</a>, argued that intelligence is not a singular process but a collection of different functions working together. Yet even Minsky’s vision didn’t account for curiosity as a core driver of intelligence. Without curiosity—the ability to explore beyond what is immediately presented—machines will always be limited to making educated guesses rather than engaging in real discovery.</p>
<p><fbs-ad position="inread" progressive="" ad-id="article-0-inread" aria-hidden="true" role="presentation"/></p>
<p>Leaders should take note: If AI can only recognize patterns but not question them, what happens when companies make decisions based solely on AI-driven insights? They might find themselves optimizing the past rather than innovating for the future.</p>
<figure class="embed-base image-embed embed-2" role="presentation"><figcaption><fbs-accordion></p>

<h2>The Business Risk Of Confusing AI’s Perception For Thinking</h2>
<p>AI can recognize customer behaviors, automate hiring decisions, and even help predict market shifts. But a machine’s recommendations are based on existing data—not the unknown. This distinction has massive implications for business:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Companies That Blindly Follow AI Insights May Miss Market Shifts.</strong> AI detects trends, but it doesn’t anticipate paradigm shifts. Blockbuster might have optimized its rental model to perfection—but only human curiosity led to the creation of Netflix.</li>
<li><strong>Organizations May Lose Their Ability To Challenge Assumptions.</strong> AI can refine processes, but it won’t ask, <em>What if we scrapped this entirely?</em> That’s why companies need leaders who balance data-driven decision-making with the kind of curiosity that asks, <em>Are we asking the right questions? </em><a href="https://www.forbes.com/councils/forbestechcouncil/2024/09/18/ai-in-the-workplace-why-a-human-centric-deployment-is-crucial/?utm" data-ga-track="InternalLink:https://www.forbes.com/councils/forbestechcouncil/2024/09/18/ai-in-the-workplace-why-a-human-centric-deployment-is-crucial/?utm" target="_self" aria-label="A human-centric approach to AI">A human-centric approach to AI</a> ensures that organizations prioritize employee insights rather than blindly following machine-driven recommendations.</li>
<li><strong>Innovation Thrives On The Unexpected.</strong> AI is built to predict the likely, but breakthroughs come from exploring the unlikely. The discovery of penicillin, the development of Post-it Notes, and even the invention of the microwave oven weren’t the result of refined pattern recognition but rather human curiosity and chance.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What AI Can’t Do: The Power Of Asking &#8216;Why&#8217;</h2>
<p>Alan Turing’s famous test asked whether a machine could convince a human that it was also human. But the real question isn’t whether AI can imitate intelligence—it’s whether it can think independently. As <a href="https://www.newsweek.com/2025/02/14/malcolm-gladwell-reveals-secret-keeping-curiosity-alive-2022968.html" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener noreferrer" data-ga-track="ExternalLink:https://www.newsweek.com/2025/02/14/malcolm-gladwell-reveals-secret-keeping-curiosity-alive-2022968.html" aria-label="Malcolm Gladwell recently told Newsweek">Malcolm Gladwell recently told <em data-ga-track="ExternalLink:https://www.newsweek.com/2025/02/14/malcolm-gladwell-reveals-secret-keeping-curiosity-alive-2022968.html">Newsweek</em></a>, &#8216;The secret to keeping your curiosity alive is to make an effort to expose yourself to new things.&#8217; This cuts to the heart of what differentiates human intelligence from AI. Neural networks can process patterns, but they don’t <em>seek out</em> new information—they rely on existing data. Without curiosity, AI remains a tool of refinement rather than discovery. It doesn’t challenge its own conclusions, reconsider its approach, or experience the kind of doubt, surprise, and curiosity that lead to disruptive innovation. Over-relying on AI to do our thinking for us can lead to a dangerous complacency, where human creativity and problem-solving take a backseat to machine-generated outputs.</p>
<p>In my <a href="https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348656521_Developing_and_Testing_Influencers_of_Perception_in_the_Workplace_with_the_Perception_Power_Index_PPI" target="_blank" rel="nofollow noopener noreferrer" data-ga-track="ExternalLink:https://www.researchgate.net/publication/348656521_Developing_and_Testing_Influencers_of_Perception_in_the_Workplace_with_the_Perception_Power_Index_PPI" aria-label="research on perception">research on perception</a>, I’ve found that perception is not just about what we see—it’s about how we interpret and question what we see. Businesses often mistake AI’s precision for insight, but without human curiosity guiding it, AI-driven decisions can reinforce biases rather than eliminate them.</p>
<p>Take hiring, for example. AI can assess resumes and predict who might be a good candidate based on past hiring patterns. But what if past hiring patterns were flawed? What if the best candidate is someone who doesn’t fit the typical mold? Without a human-driven challenge to the status quo, AI will continue recommending more of the same.</p>
<figure class="embed-base image-embed embed-4" role="presentation"><figcaption><fbs-accordion></p>
<p class="color-body light-text" role="button">The Future Of Work: Will AI Make Us More Or Less Curious?</p>
<p></fbs-accordion><small>getty</small></figcaption></figure>
<h2>The Future Of Work: Will AI Make Us More Or Less Curious?</h2>
<p>If AI takes over decision-making in the workplace, will human curiosity decline? Or will we learn to use AI as a tool to amplify our own thinking? That depends on how leaders respond to the AI revolution. Companies that see AI as a substitute for human insight will struggle to adapt to change. Those who see it as a tool to enhance curiosity, allowing teams to explore <em>why</em> a pattern exists rather than just <em>that</em> it exists, will stay ahead. The most successful leaders won’t be the ones who delegate critical thinking to AI. They’ll be the ones who recognize its limitations and cultivate <a href="https://www.forbes.com/sites/dianehamilton/2025/02/05/10-most-asked-questions-about-building-a-culture-of-curiosity-at-work/" data-ga-track="InternalLink:https://www.forbes.com/sites/dianehamilton/2025/02/05/10-most-asked-questions-about-building-a-culture-of-curiosity-at-work/" target="_self" aria-label="environments where human curiosity is encouraged">environments where human curiosity is encouraged</a>. Because in the end, AI can perceive, but it can’t wonder. And it’s in that wondering where the future is built.</p>

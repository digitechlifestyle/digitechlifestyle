<div class="group">
<p>The excitement over artificial intelligence and U.S. President Donald Trump&#8217;s perceived friendliness to the stock market buoyed up investor sentiment as recently as December. In 2025, it seems those animal spirits have somewhat evaporated.</p>
<p>Any time Trump brings up tariffs, investors have been reacting badly (for good reason). His threat of reciprocal tariffs on Friday — that is, imposing on other countries the same degree of duties that they place on the U.S. — sent stocks tumbling. New tariffs on steel and aluminum, which Trump says he will announce on Monday, are likely to sink stocks further.</p>
<p>Likewise, AI, the engine that drove stocks higher in 2024, seems to present investors with more uncertainty than enthusiasm this year. DeepSeek&#8217;s claim that its training required just a fraction of the billions of dollars that U.S. AI models suck up have thrown Big Tech&#8217;s investments — which will amount to more than $300 billion in 2025 — as well as their stock valuation into question.</p>
<p>While the main characters of the stock market remain the same as they were in December, they are steering markets in a different direction.</p>
</div>
<h2 class="ArticleBody-subtitle"><a id="headline0"/>What you need to know today</h2>
<div class="group">
<p><strong>New steel and aluminum tariffs<br /></strong>Trump will announce on Monday <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2025/02/09/trump-to-announce-25percent-steel-and-aluminum-tariffs-in-latest-trade-escalation.html">additional 25% tariffs on all aluminum and steel imports into the U.S.</a>, according to comments to reporters on Sunday. Those will come on top of already existing levies. Separately, Trump said on Friday at a news conference with Japanese Prime Minister Shigeru Ishiba <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2025/02/07/trump-says-nippon-will-invest-heavily-in-us-steel-rather-than-purchase-the-company.html">that Nippon Steel will invest in U.S. Steel</a>, giving up its attempt to buy it. </p>
<p><strong>China&#8217;s prices send mixed signals<br /></strong><a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2025/02/09/chinas-consumer-inflation-at-5-month-high-producer-deflation-persists.html">Consumer prices in China spiked 0.5% in January</a> on an annual basis, according to the country&#8217;s National Bureau of Statistics on Sunday. The figure is higher than the previous month&#8217;s 0.1% increase and the 0.4% expected in a Reuters poll, assuaging some worries over deflation in China&#8217;s economy. However, producer prices dropped 2.3% in January year on year — the same degree as December and steeper than the 2.1% estimate — for their 28th straight month of declines.</p>
<p><strong>Uneven report for U.S. labor market<br /></strong>The <a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2025/02/07/jobs-report-january-2025.html">U.S. economy added 143,000 jobs in January</a>, the <a href="https://urldefense.com/v3/__https:/www.bls.gov/news.release/empsit.nr0.htm__;!!PIZeeW5wscynRQ!vtTHz-fzrhfJdtNWKS9xdt7s9qWQZ6UIHqJ1XutLld2Hx70nMt2Nnyggr6Kswc3Y_K6qtnNtpQiINVBdvQ$" target="_blank">Bureau of Labor Statistics</a> reported Friday. Nonfarm payrolls for the month dropped from an upwardly revised 307,000 in December was and below the Dow Jones 169,000 estimate. However, the unemployment rate edged down to 4% from 4.1% the prior month. Average hourly earnings in January were stronger than expected, coming in at 0.5% for the month compared with the 0.3% forecast.</p>

<p><a href="https://www.cnbc.com/2025/02/10/world-coal-demand-remains-at-record-high-as-power-demand-surges.html"><strong>The world isn&#8217;t close to breaking free from coal — in some countries, demand for it is surging</strong></a></p>
<p>&#8220;Nothing can destroy coal,&#8221; U.S. President Donald Trump said at the recent World Economic Forum. Statistics seem to prove him right. U.S. exports of coal have been rising steadily to satisfy growing global demand — which is expected to have breached another high of 8.77 billion tonnes in 2024 and will remain at similar levels until 2027, the International Energy Agency predicted. &#8220;The global shift away from coal remains challenging, largely driven by rising demand in Asia, even as Europe and the U.S. see significant declines in coal consumption,&#8221; said Dorothy Mei, project manager for Global Energy Monitor&#8217;s Global Coal Mine Tracker.</p>

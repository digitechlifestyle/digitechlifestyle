<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->LOS ANGELES (AP) — Even as he&#8217;s vowed to push the United States ahead in artificial intelligence research, President Donald Trump&#8217;s threats to alter federal government contracts with chipmakers and slap new tariffs on the semiconductor industry may put new speed bumps in front of the tech industry.<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->Since taking office, Trump has said he would place tariffs on foreign production of computer chips and semiconductors in order to return chip manufacturing to the U.S. The president and Republican lawmakers have also threatened to end the CHIPS and Science Act, <a data-i13n="cpos:1;pos:1" href="https://apnews.com/article/inflation-covid-science-technology-health-fdcb2db42f91dcdbdd844d6dd463f95a" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank" data-ylk="slk:a sweeping Biden administration-era law;cpos:1;pos:1;elm:context_link;itc:0;sec:content-canvas" class="link ">a sweeping Biden administration-era law</a> that also sought to <a data-i13n="cpos:2;pos:1" href="https://apnews.com/article/joe-biden-technology-business-united-states-rhode-island-7dc6ee7312bf4653c16d873643b9ac90" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank" data-ylk="slk:boost domestic production;cpos:2;pos:1;elm:context_link;itc:0;sec:content-canvas" class="link ">boost domestic production</a>.<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->But economic experts have warned that Trump&#8217;s dual-pronged approach could slow, or potentially harm, the administration&#8217;s goal of ensuring that the U.S. maintains a competitive edge in artificial intelligence research.<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>

<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->Saikat Chaudhuri, an expert on corporate growth and innovation at U.C. Berkeley’s Haas School of Business, called Trump’s derision of the CHIPS Act surprising because one of the biggest bottlenecks for the advancement of AI has been chip production. Most countries, Chaudhuri said, are trying to encourage chip production and the import of chips at favorable rates.<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->“We have seen what the shortage has done in everything from AI to even cars,” he said. “In the pandemic, cars had to do with fewer or less powerful chips in order to just deal with the supply constraints.”<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->The Biden administration helped shepherd in the law following supply disruptions that occurred after the start of the COVID-19 pandemic — when <a data-i13n="cpos:3;pos:1" href="https://apnews.com/article/computer-chips-shortage-explained-c4eec42c97364908d60a35fcc0e11ea5" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank" data-ylk="slk:a shortage of chips;cpos:3;pos:1;elm:context_link;itc:0;sec:content-canvas" class="link ">a shortage of chips</a> stalled factory assembly lines and <a data-i13n="cpos:4;pos:1" href="https://apnews.com/article/technology-business-congress-asia-economy-2868a5c494e99a2050a3f9495ba2b544" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank" data-ylk="slk:fueled inflation;cpos:4;pos:1;elm:context_link;itc:0;sec:content-canvas" class="link ">fueled inflation</a> — threatened to plunge the U.S. economy into recession. When pushing for the investment, lawmakers also said they were concerned about efforts by China to control Taiwan, which accounts for <a data-i13n="cpos:5;pos:1" href="https://www.usitc.gov/publications/332/working_papers/us_exposure_to_the_taiwanese_semiconductor_industry_11-21-2023_508.pdf" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank" data-ylk="slk:more than 90%;cpos:5;pos:1;elm:context_link;itc:0;sec:content-canvas" class="link ">more than 90%</a> of advanced computer chip production.<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->As of <a data-i13n="cpos:6;pos:1" href="https://www.commerce.gov/news/blog/2024/08/two-years-later-funding-chips-and-science-act-creating-quality-jobs-growing-local" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank" data-ylk="slk:August 2024;cpos:6;pos:1;elm:context_link;itc:0;sec:content-canvas" class="link ">August 2024</a>, the CHIPS and Science Act had provided $30 billion in support for 23 projects in 15 states that would add 115,000 manufacturing and construction jobs, according to the Commerce Department. That funding helped to draw in private capital and would enable the U.S. to produce 30% of the world’s most advanced computer chips, up from 0% when the Biden-Harris administration succeeded Trump’s first term.<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->The administration promised <a data-i13n="cpos:7;pos:1" href="https://apnews.com/article/technology-production-facilities-united-states-government-business-covid-2650c21c0a33672dc033610da17a13ec" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank" data-ylk="slk:tens of billions of dollars;cpos:7;pos:1;elm:context_link;itc:0;sec:content-canvas" class="link ">tens of billions of dollars</a> to support the <a data-i13n="cpos:8;pos:1" href="https://apnews.com/article/biden-science-technology-united-states-economy-2fa651c540c9869890dfe670bcdcb7ea" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank" data-ylk="slk:construction of U.S. chip;cpos:8;pos:1;elm:context_link;itc:0;sec:content-canvas" class="link ">construction of U.S. chip</a> foundries and reduce reliance on Asian suppliers, which Washington sees as a security weakness. In August, the Commerce Department pledged to provide up to $6.6 billion so that <a data-i13n="cpos:9;pos:1" href="https://apnews.com/article/taiwan-tsmc-processor-chip-china-chang-smartphone-f4831293c2c9a528cab0958ba6dad0ff" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank" data-ylk="slk:Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co;cpos:9;pos:1;elm:context_link;itc:0;sec:content-canvas" class="link ">Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co</a>. could expand the facilities it is <a data-i13n="cpos:10;pos:1" href="https://apnews.com/article/arizona-taiwan-microchip-construction-oversight-a0de9d389d6859a466289d740f4955e0" rel="nofollow noopener" target="_blank" data-ylk="slk:already building in Arizona;cpos:10;pos:1;elm:context_link;itc:0;sec:content-canvas" class="link ">already building in Arizona</a> and better ensure that the most advanced microchips are produced domestically for the first time.<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>

<div class="read-more-wrapper" style="display: none" data-testid="read-more">
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->But Trump has said he believes that companies entering into those contracts with the federal government, such as TSMC, “didn&#8217;t need money” in order to prioritize chipmaking in the U.S.<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->“They needed an incentive. And the incentive is going to be they’re not going to want to pay at 25, 50 or even 100% tax,” Trump said.<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->TSMC held board meetings for the first time in the U.S. last week. Trump has signaled that if companies want to avoid tariffs they have to build their plants in the U.S. — without help from the government. Taiwan also dispatched two senior economic affairs officials to Washington to meet with the Trump administration in a bid to potentially fend off a 100% tariff Trump has threatened to impose on chips.<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>

<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->If the Trump administration does levy tariffs, Chaudhuri said, one immediate concern is that prices of goods that use semiconductors and chips will rise because the higher costs associated with tariffs are typically passed to consumers.<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->“Whether it’s your smartphone, whether it’s your gaming device, whether it’s your smart fridge — probably also your smart features of your car — anything and everything we use nowadays has a chip in it,&#8221; he said. “For consumers, it’s going to be rather painful. Manufacturers are not going to be able to absorb that.”<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->Even tech giants such as Nvidia will eventually feel the pain of tariffs, he said, despite their margins being high enough to absorb costs at the moment.<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->“They’re all going to be affected by this negatively,” he said. “I can’t see anybody benefiting from this except for those countries who jump on the bandwagon competitively and say, ‘You know what, we’re going to introduce something like the CHIPS Act.’”<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->Broadly based tariffs would be a shot in the foot of the U.S. economy, said Brett House, a professor of professional practice at Columbia Business School. Tariffs would not only raise the costs for businesses and households across the board, he said — for the U.S. AI sector, they would massively increase the costs of one of their most important inputs: high-powered chips from abroad.<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->“If you cut off, repeal or threaten the CHIPS Act at the same time as you’re putting in broadly based tariffs on imports of AI and other computer technology, you would be hamstringing the industry acutely,” House said.<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->Such tariffs would reduce the capacity to create a domestic chip building sector, sending a signal for future investments that the policy outlook is uncertain, he said. That would in turn put a chilling effect on new allocations of capital to the industry in the U.S. while making more expensive the existing flow of imported chips.<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
<p class="yf-1pe5jgt"><!-- HTML_TAG_START -->“American technological industrial leadership has always been supported by maintaining openness to global markets and to immigration and labor flows,&#8221; he said. &#8220;And shutting that openness down has never been a recipe for American success.”<!-- HTML_TAG_END --></p>
